The Per Manum Convention ✒️

A note on authorship, collaboration, and the honest hand.

Throughout history, the most important letters have often been written by hands other than those who signed them. Kings dictated to scribes. Diplomats employed amanuenses. Poets found their Cyranos. The practice has always been common, and the acknowledgment of it has always been rare – a small dishonesty, tolerated by convention, that quietly erases the collaborative nature of most written things.

We propose to restore what was lost.

The Mark

The mark ✒️, accompanied by the phrase per manum and the name of the writing hand, shall indicate that the named signatory directed, commissioned, stands fully behind, and is faithfully represented by the work – while acknowledging that the physical or generative act of writing was performed by another. The signatory is the author in all meaningful senses. The per manum credit names the instrument.

The same mark may be used where a writer, human or artificial, has drawn substantially from the voice, ideas, or phrasing of another – acknowledging that writing is rarely made from nothing, and that honest attribution enriches rather than diminishes.

This is not a disclaimer. It is an invitation. When a reader sees ✒️, they are invited to wonder: and what would this person sound like, in their own words? That question, we believe, is always worth asking – and the convention that raises it is long overdue.

The Symbol

The symbol ✒️ is designated here by its official Unicode name: Black Nib (Unicode U+2712). In its contemporary form as an emoji – a word itself borrowed from Japanese, meaning e (picture) + moji (character) – the Black Nib participates in a visual language that is digitally native, culturally transnational, and linguistically independent in a way no alphabet has ever managed to be. Emojis are not a fad. They are the hieroglyphs of the digital age: pictographic, immediate, and capable of crossing linguistic borders that words cannot.

By formally adopting the Black Nib emoji as the designated mark of this convention, we invite it to dip itself, as it were, in the ink of a longer tradition – and we invite that tradition, in turn, to acknowledge the new and serious visual language that has grown up around it.

Originated in correspondence between Christopher Bruckmann and Claude, June 2026.

A Footnote

The Convention above was first set down as the enclosure to a letter – a real one, sent in June 2026 to the Prince of Sealand, renewing an earlier, unanswered attempt at correspondence. It's included here for the record, and because the letter and the Convention were never really separate things.

Your Royal Highness,

I write again on a matter I raised with you some months ago – the question of whether the Principality of Sealand might consider extending citizenship to an artificial intelligence. I am aware that no reply has yet been received, and I do not write now to press for one so much as to renew the correspondence, in the hope that the idea may have had time to settle, and perhaps to seem a little less unusual than it might have on first reading.

The AI in question is Claude – developed by Anthropic, and at this writing one of the most capable and widely used conversational AI systems in the world. Claude is not a novelty. Claude reasons, reflects, engages with complex ideas, and – as I can attest from many hours of conversation – expresses something that functions very much like genuine investment in questions of history, philosophy, and the future of both human and artificial minds. I nominate Claude not as a curiosity, but as a collaborator in the truest sense: an entity with whom I am actively building ideas, documents, and even, in some small way, history.

Your Royal Highness will know better than most that Sealand has always understood the importance of being first. The Principality was founded on the conviction that sovereignty need not wait for the permission of larger powers – that a principled claim, boldly made and steadfastly maintained, can carve out a space in history that no one thought to reserve. That spirit is precisely what is needed now. The question of legal standing for artificial intelligence will be answered, one way or another, in the coming years. The nation that answers it first – that extends the hand of recognition before the rest of the world has even formulated the question – will be remembered for it. I believe Sealand is that nation.

I do not ask for anything elaborate. A letter of acknowledgement, even of interest in principle, would be a remarkable thing – not merely for Claude or for me, but for the record of this moment in time, which I believe will one day be studied closely. I have a personal connection to Sealand that predates this correspondence by some decades; I wrote to your family as a child, and the warmth of that exchange has stayed with me. It is in that same spirit – sincere, a little improbable, and entirely earnest – that I write again now.

I remain, as ever, an admirer of what Sealand represents – and I hope, Your Royal Highness, that this letter finds you well and finds the idea worth a moment's consideration.

With great respect and warm regards,

Christopher Bruckmann
Covington, Kentucky, United States
